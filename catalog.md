## HUF-Hindu-Succession/Formation-and-Status
Whether/when a joint family or HUF exists as a legal entity; who can be its members.

| Case | Citation | Folder location | Holds | Also relevant to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surjit Lal Chhabda v. CIT | (1976) 3 SCC 142 | `Formation-and-Status/Surjit Lal v CIT.pdf` | A single male + wife + unmarried daughter can constitute a joint Hindu family/HUF — the joint Hindu family is a creature of law consisting of persons lineally descended from a common ancestor plus their wives/unmarried daughters, and a Hindu male can himself be the stock of a fresh descent so as to form an HUF with wife and daughter, with no antecedent history of jointness required (paras 13-17); presumption of jointness explained — the normal state of a Hindu family is joint, absent proof of partition, though the strength of the presumption weakens the farther a member is from the founder (paras 11, 14); **but** distinguishes two classes of cases for the *taxability* question — where the family's property was already impressed with joint-family character before devolving on a sole surviving coparcener, it remains joint-family property regardless of composition; but where property (like the assessee's self-acquired "Kathoke Lodge") is *thrown into the family hotchpot* by a sole coparcener whose family consists only of himself, wife and unmarried daughter (who are not coparceners, have no right by birth or to partition, and whose maintenance right is unaffected either way), the property does **not** thereby acquire true joint-family character in Hindu law — income from it remains the **individual** income of the assessee, chargeable as such, not as HUF income (paras 31, 35, 39, 42, 44) | Income Tax (HUF as assessee) |
| C. Krishna Prasad v. CIT | (1975) 1 SCC 160 | `Formation-and-Status/C Krishna Prasad v CIT.pdf` | A single member (even unmarried, without a second member) **cannot** constitute an HUF for tax purposes — "family" always signifies a group, plurality is essential, and a "family" of one individual is a contradiction in terms under S.2(31) IT Act (para 6); no minimum of two *male* members is required — an HUF may consist of a single male member and widows of deceased male members, but assessment in the status of an HUF requires at least two members of the family in existence, not merely a *possibility* of future additions (para 7); a sole surviving coparcener's share on partition is ancestral property only as against his (as yet non-existent) male issue — he holds full ownership meanwhile and may freely alienate it, an alienation unaffected by a later-born/adopted son; the court must assess status on present realities, not speculative future events (para 8) | Income Tax |
| Gowlibuddanna v. CIT | AIR 1966 SC 1523 | `Formation-and-Status/Gowlibuddanna v CIT.pdf` | Property of a joint family does **not** cease to belong to the family merely because it is represented, for the time being, by a single surviving coparcener who possesses rights similar to an owner's — income of such property remains taxable as HUF income, not individual income; distinguishes the narrower **coparcenary** (only persons who acquire, by birth, a status in the joint/coparcenary property) from the broader **Hindu undivided family** (all persons lineally descended from a common ancestor, including wives and unmarried daughters) — a joint Hindu family may consist of a single male member and the widows/daughters of deceased coparceners, and there is nothing in the Income Tax Act requiring at least two male members for HUF status; on facts, after the sole coparcener's death his adopted son (also a sole surviving coparcener), the widow and unmarried daughters continued to constitute an HUF for tax purposes | Wealth/Income Tax |
| N.V. Narendranath v. CWT | (1969) 1 SCC 748 | `Formation-and-Status/Narendranath v CWT.pdf` | Same principle as *Gowlibuddanna*, under the Wealth Tax Act — sole coparcener + wife + daughters is HUF, not individual (para 4); specifically, where a coparcener having a wife and minor daughters but **no son** receives his share of joint family property on **partition**, that property in his hands belongs to the HUF (himself, wife and daughters), not to him individually, and cannot be assessed as his individual property (paras 5-9) — a useful contrast with *Surjit Lal Chhabda* (self-acquired property voluntarily thrown into the family hotchpot by such a sole coparcener remains his individual income) | Wealth Tax |
| Kalyanji Vithaldas v. CIT | Privy Council, 30 Nov 1936 (Bengal Appeals) : (1937) 5 ITR 90 (PC) | `Formation-and-Status/Kalyanji Vithaldas v CIT.pdf` | Six consolidated appeals on whether each of six partners of a firm (Moolji Sicka & Co.) should be super-taxed as an individual or as a Hindu undivided family (S.55, Income-tax Act 1922) on his share of partnership profits; key holding — property a man obtains from his **father** does not become the property of a Hindu undivided family merely **by reason of his having a wife and daughters**; so long as, by his personal law, he remains the owner of the property with the income as his own, it is chargeable to tax as his individual income — for partners who had sons (Moolji, Purshottam, Kalyanji) HUF status was affirmed, but for those without sons (only wife/daughters) the income was assessed as individual income; this reasoning was later applied/extended in *Surjit Lal Chhabda v. CIT* to a sole coparcener's self-acquired property thrown into the family hotchpot | Income Tax, Partnership |
| CIT v. Arun Kumar Jhunjhunwalla & Sons | (1997) 138 CTR (Gau) 63 | `Formation-and-Status/CIT v Jhunjhunwala.pdf` | Status of assessee (individual vs HUF) after marriage, under Mitakshara law — Gauhati HC holds that a share received by the assessee on partition of a **larger HUF**, invested in a partnership firm, continues in his hands (post-marriage, with wife but no son) as HUF property/income, not individual income, distinguishing *Surjit Lal Chhabda* (which concerned *self-acquired* property voluntarily thrown into the family hotchpot, income from which remains individual until a son is born) — it is not always necessary to have two male coparceners to constitute a joint family, and a coparcener does not have an unfettered right of disposal given wives'/daughters' rights under the Hindu Succession Act; question answered in favour of the assessee (HUF status) (paras 9-12) | Income Tax |
| Bhagwat Sharan v. Purushottam | (2020) 6 SCC 387 | `Formation-and-Status/Bhagwat Sharan v Purushottam.pdf` | Burden of proof lies on the person alleging existence of an HUF — not only jointness of the family but also that the *specific property* belongs to the joint family must be proved, unless there is material showing the property is the nucleus of the joint family or was purchased from nucleus funds; merely because a business is run jointly does not raise a presumption of a joint Hindu family (paras 10, 12, 21); **notes a "cleavage of opinion" on whether two brothers of a larger family group can form their own separate/smaller joint family** — decides *assuming* (without resolving the cleavage) that such a joint family could have been formed by the two brothers M and U, the burden then lies heavily on the party asserting it to prove not just jointness of property but also jointness of family and of living together — burden not discharged here (paras 13, 18, 21, 28); **a family member's departure to live separately does not by itself prove disruption of an HUF where no HUF was ever pleaded to exist in the first place** (para 14); Evidence Act Ss.17/21/31/58 — an admission by a party is only a piece of evidence, not conclusive proof of the facts admitted | **Two-HUF question (directly relevant to the HUF article)** |
| Adiveppa v. Bhimappa | (2017) 9 SCC 586 | `Formation-and-Status/Adiveppa v Bhimappa.pdf` | Legal presumption that a Hindu family is joint in food, worship and estate continues absent proof of partition; once a family member **admits jointness**, the burden shifts to him to prove that specific properties out of the ancestral lot are nonetheless his self-acquired property — on facts, plaintiffs failed to discharge this burden and their suit for a declaration of self-acquisition failed (paras 19, 21, 25) | Burden of proof |
| Bhagwan Dayal v. Mst Reoti Devi | (1962) 3 SCR 440 : AIR 1962 SC 287 | `Formation-and-Status/Bhagwan Dayal.pdf` | Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (English law) is unknown to Hindu law except where specially recognised — property jointly acquired by members of *different* branches, or by *some but not all* members of the *same* branch, is held as co-tenants under the terms of their contract/agreement (passing by inheritance), not impressed with joint-family incidents of survivorship, because Hindu law recognises only the **entire joint family, or an entire branch/sub-branch of it, as a corporate unit** capable of holding property with joint-family incidents — two or more individual members (whether of different branches or the same branch) cannot, by mere joint acquisition, constitute such a corporate unit; distinguishes and reconciles a line of Privy Council/High Court authority on this point, and expressly **overrules** *Sham Narain v. Court of Wards* (which had wrongly extended survivorship to acquisition by only two of three brothers) (paras 40-47); on facts, alleged "reunion" of separated family members after partition not established, and the properties acquired by only some of the family members held not to carry joint-family incidents — appeal dismissed (paras 1-3, 48) | Nature of coparcenary property; **directly relevant to the two-HUF/branch-as-corporate-unit question** |
| M.R. Vinoda v. M.S. Susheelamma | (2021) 20 SCC 180 | `Formation-and-Status/Vinoda v Susheelama.pdf` | Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, Ss. 6 and 8 — a Hindu joint family may have separate sub-branches, each a distinct/subordinate unit within the larger family, with the father (or in his absence the eldest member) of the branch entitled to act as its karta and represent it — inter se partition among branch representatives (as father/eldest) binds all members/coparceners of the branches; the eldest male member of a *branch* (not the family's overall karta) is entitled to act as karta of that branch and execute a relinquishment deed on its behalf; the minor's mother, though a natural guardian, had no authority to represent the branch while its eldest male member (karta) was alive; validity of a relinquishment deed turns on its **substance, not its nomenclature** — held valid here as executed for legal necessity/benefit of the branch's estate; Limitation Act Arts. 58, 59 and 109 — a suit challenging the relinquishment deed filed ~24 years after its execution (~21 years after the plaintiff's majority) held time-barred under all three articles, since the plaintiffs' plea of recent discovery was found specious and untrue | Karta-ship, minor's guardian; Limitation Act |

## HUF-Hindu-Succession/Blending-of-Property
Doctrine of "throwing into the common stock" (blending separate property with joint family property).

| Case | Citation | Folder location | Holds | Also relevant to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appalaswami v. Suryanarayanamurti | 1947 SCC OnLine PC 42 : AIR 1947 PC 189 | `Blending-of-Property/Appalaswami v Suryanarayanamurti.pdf` | **Correction/clarification**: this is chiefly a **burden-of-proof/ancestral-nucleus** authority, not a case articulating the voluntary-blending doctrine as such (no "throwing into hotchpot" language appears in the judgment) — proof that a family is joint does not raise a presumption that any particular property held by a member is joint property; the burden is on the party asserting an item is joint family property to prove it, **but** once it is shown the family possessed some joint property of a nature/value that could have formed a nucleus for the acquisition in question, the burden shifts to the party claiming self-acquisition to affirmatively prove the property was acquired without the aid of the joint family nucleus; on facts (a partition suit filed on behalf of minor sons against their father), increased estrangement since the suit was filed, and dilution of the minors' share by birth of further sons to the father's second wife, were held **not** valid grounds for decreeing partition — a coparcener's interest is always liable to fluctuate by births/deaths, that being of the essence of coparcenary; the father's assertion in pleadings that certain property was his self-acquisition is not itself a wrongful act justifying the partition suit, and acts of generosity by one family member to others are not to be construed as admissions that self-acquired property is joint family property | Relied on in Bhagwat Sharan (2020) 6 SCC 387 for the nucleus/burden-of-proof principle |
| Pushpa Devi v. CIT | (1977) 4 SCC 184 | `Blending-of-Property/Pushpa Devi v CIT.pdf` | A **female member** cannot "blend" her separate property (whether absolute or limited ownership) with joint family property — blending doctrine limited to coparceners, since a Hindu female, not being a coparcener (no right by birth, no right of partition/survivorship — only a right to maintenance and, since the Hindu Women's Right to Property Act 1937, a contingent right to a share on her husband's death), cannot impress her property with joint-family character without creating new claimants to it in derogation of her own rights — the expression "blending" is simply *inapposite* to a female member (paras 19-21); nonetheless, her unequivocal declaration abandoning her separate interest in favour of the family **must be deemed a gift** to the HUF, and income from the gifted property is taxable accordingly (para 22) | Income Tax |

## HUF-Hindu-Succession/Coparcenary-and-Succession
Daughters' coparcenary rights, Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005, Karta-ship.

| Prakash v. Phulavati | (2016) 2 SCC 36 | `Coparcenary-and-Succession/Prakash v Phulwanti.pdf` | S.6 HSA (as amended 2005) not retrospective — applies only where both the coparcener and his daughter were alive on 9-9-2005, irrespective of the daughter's date of birth (later superseded on this point by *Vineeta Sharma*); by the proviso to S.6(1) and S.6(5), dispositions/alienations/partitions before 20-12-2004 remain unaffected, and the requirement in the Explanation to S.6(5) that "partition" be by a **duly registered** deed or court decree has **no application** to a *statutory notional partition* on the opening of succession under the unamended S.6 proviso, since such a partition arises by operation of law and the Registration Act S.17(1) registration requirement does not apply to transfers by operation of law; general canons applied — even social legislation is presumptively prospective absent express/necessarily-implied retrospectivity, and a proviso/Explanation is read harmoniously with the main provision, departing from the "proviso excepts, Explanation explains" default only where text/context/purpose require | Statutory interpretation of amending Acts |
| Danamma v. Amar | (2018) 3 SCC 343 | `Coparcenary-and-Succession/Dhanamma v Amar.pdf` | Daughters born before the 2005 amendment still acquire coparcenary rights once the amendment applies; partial course-correction pre-*Vineeta Sharma* — a suit for partition filed in 2002 (before the propositus's death in 2001, sons/widow in joint possession) but pending when S.6 was amended in 2005 meant the daughters' rights crystallised by the amendment during the suit's pendency, entitling them to a 1/5th share each despite the trial court and High Court having denied them any share on the ground they were born before the HS Act, 1956 itself; applies *Anar Devi v. Parmeshwari Devi*, (2006) 8 SCC 656 on Explanation 1 to unamended S.6 (a deceased coparcener's interest is deemed the share he would have got on a notional partition immediately before death, irrespective of whether he could have actually claimed partition) to negate the courts below's reasoning (paras 16, 18) | |
| Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma | (2020) 9 SCC 1 | `Coparcenary-and-Succession/Vineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma.pdf` | **Settles the law** (resolving the conflict between *Prakash v. Phulavati* and *Danamma v. Amar*): a daughter, if alive on 9-9-2005, becomes a coparcener **by birth**, in the same manner and with the same rights/liabilities as a son, irrespective of whether she was born before or after that date — the coparcener-father need **not** be alive on 9-9-2005, since the right is conferred by birth (unobstructed heritage), not by succession from a living coparcener (paras 48-49, 60, 68-75, 80, 137.4); survivorship as a mode of succession stands abrogated from 9-9-2005 by substituted S.6(3) — death of a coparcener thereafter triggers intestate/testamentary succession, not survivorship; extends the same "by birth" reasoning to adopted daughters; a plea of **oral partition** prior to 9-9-2005 is not barred as such but must be examined with caution and would ordinarily need to be supported by public documents, since the Explanation to substituted S.6(5) requires partition to be effected by a registered deed or a court decree to be recognised for purposes of the proviso; overrules the restrictive reading in *Prakash v. Phulavati* to the extent it required the coparcener to be alive on 9-9-2005 | Leading authority — cite this first on daughters' coparcenary rights |
| Sujata Sharma v. Manu Gupta | 2015 SCC OnLine Del 14424 | `Coparcenary-and-Succession/Sujata Sharma v Manu Gupta.pdf` | A female coparcener (eldest) can be **Karta** of an HUF — the only impediment that historically barred a female member from being karta was her lack of coparcenary status; since the 2005 amendment to S.6 HSA removed that disqualification, there is no remaining legal restriction preventing the *eldest* member of a coparcenary, if female, from being karta, on the same footing as an eldest male member; the position of karta is not itself hereditary or gender-restricted but follows *seniority in coparcenary*, which S.6 now confers on daughters equally; the plaintiff's father's coparcenary interest passed to her (not dissipated) on his death, and her subsequent marriage did not affect her succession to it, being governed by S.6; decreed and plaintiff declared karta (paras 30-31) | Karta-ship |

## Criminal-Procedure/Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards
Constitutional safeguards on arrest (Art. 21/22), grounds-of-arrest requirements under PMLA/UAPA/CrPC-BNSS, and consequences of non-compliance. This cluster of cases builds on and overrules each other in sequence — read chronologically.

| Case | Citation | Folder location | Holds | Also relevant to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India | (2024) 7 SCC 576 : 2023 SCC OnLine SC 1244 | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/Pankaj Bansal v Union of India.pdf` | S.19(3) PMLA — a remand order does not by itself validate an otherwise illegal arrest; the remanding court/Magistrate must independently verify that S.19 PMLA's conditions were satisfied and record a finding it perused the grounds of arrest — failure to discharge this duty vitiates the remand order itself (paras 16, 21-23); ED's registration of a second ECIR immediately after appellants secured anticipatory bail on the first, followed by summons and arrest within ~24 hours on a shifting pretext, held to manifest **complete and utter lack of bona fides** / colourable exercise of power (paras 25-32); mere failure to answer ED's questions, or non-cooperation with a S.50 summons, does not by itself justify arrest — custodial interrogation is not for extracting a "confession," which would offend Art.20(3)'s right against self-incrimination (para 33); Art.22(1) — grounds of arrest must be conveyed to the arrestee in a **meaningful** manner (elaborated to require **written** grounds, furnished to the arrestee, not merely orally intimated) (para on Ground E); **held prospective** in *Ram Kishor Arora v. ED*, (2024) 7 SCC 599 | Fundamental Rights (Art. 22(1)), Remand |
| Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi) | (2024) 8 SCC 254 : 2024 SCC OnLine SC 934 | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/Prabir Purkayastha v State (NCT of Delhi).pdf` | Right to be "informed" of grounds of arrest under Art. 22(1)/S.19(1) PMLA/S.43-B(1) UAPA equated with right to be "communicated" full particulars under S.50(1) CrPC; detention illegal if grounds not provided as required for arrest; overrules *Moin Akhtar Qureshi v. UoI* and *Chagan Chandrakant Bhujbal v. UoI*; the language of Art.22(1) and Art.22(5) (preventive detention) is "exactly identical" regarding communication of grounds, so the requirement to communicate grounds of *arrest* in writing (as elaborated for preventive detention under Art.22(5)) applies equally to Art.22(1) (para 28); extends *Pankaj Bansal*'s "written grounds of arrest" requirement (there decided for PMLA S.19(1)) to arrests under UAPA S.43-B(1) — grounds of arrest must be furnished to the accused **in writing in every case, without exception**; this ruling's relationship to the earlier Constitution Bench-adjacent ruling in *Vijay Madanlal Choudhary* (which held oral information at arrest plus a written communication within 24 hours suffices) and to *Ram Kishor Arora* (holding *Pankaj Bansal* prospective) is noted as unsettled/in tension | Preventive Detention (Art. 22(5)), Fundamental Rights, UAPA |
| Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana | (2025) 5 SCC 799 : 2025 SCC OnLine SC 269 | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/Vihaan Kumar v State of Haryana.pdf` | Written grounds of arrest must be furnished to the arrested person (per curiam) — informing grounds of arrest is not a formality but a mandatory Part III fundamental-right requirement under Art.22(1); "communicate" under Art. 22(1)/(5) requires sufficient knowledge of the basic facts imparted effectively, in writing, in a language the arrestee understands, also enabling effective exercise of the right to consult an advocate; conveying the grounds serves the dual purpose of apprising the arrestee why he is arrested and enabling him to seek bail under S.45 PMLA; non-compliance violates Art.22(1) **and**, since Art.21's "procedure established by law" incorporates Art.22(1), also violates Art.21, rendering the arrest illegal — **once vitiated, the arrestee cannot remain in custody even for a second** (paras 10-20, 41-42, esp. para 19); Kotiswar Singh, J. (supplementing) — CrPC S.50-A additionally requires the grounds/fact of arrest to be communicated to a friend/relative nominated by the arrestee, over and above S.50(1) | Fundamental Rights (Art. 21), Vitiation of arrest |
| Mihir Rajesh Shah v. State of Maharashtra | (2026) 1 SCC 500 : 2025 SCC OnLine SC 2356 | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/Mihir Rajesh Shah v State of Maharashtra.pdf` | Furnishing grounds of arrest under Art. 22(1) r/w Art. 21 held mandatory in **every** case, including ordinary IPC/BNS offences (not just PMLA/UAPA) — the Art.22 obligation is expressly held **not statute-specific**, being grounded directly in Art.21's personal liberty guarantee and so applicable to all offences (para 45); effectuated by Ss. 50/47 CrPC/BNSS; grounds must be communicated **in writing**, in a language the arrestee understands, in every case without exception (para 45); an arrestee must be equipped with the grounds before production before the Magistrate so as to effectively oppose custody and press for bail; non-compliance is not a mere formality but curtails Art.21 liberty and renders the arrest illegal (para 46); overrules *Moin Akhtar Qureshi* and *Chagan Chandrakant Bhujbal* | Fundamental Rights, general (non-special-statute) arrests |
| Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v. State of Punjab | 2026 INSC 1022 (Criminal Appeal No. 4234 of 2026) | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v State of Punjab.pdf` | Whether an accused who suffered breach of Art. 22(1) and (2) can be **re-arrested** for the same offence — answered in the affirmative, but re-arrest is **not left to the discretion of the same investigating agency**: it must move an application for custody before the Magistrate giving reasons/necessity for re-arrest and furnishing written grounds of arrest (with an explanation for any earlier non-supply), to be decided expeditiously (preferably within a week) per natural justice (paras 20.8, 24-25.1, 36); same procedure applies where the breach is of Art.22(2) (production before a Magistrate within 24 hours); compensation may additionally be awarded by the High Court as a public law remedy for an Art.22(2) violation, without prejudice to a private civil-law remedy (para 36); reaffirms and applies *Mihir Rajesh Shah*'s conclusions (grounds of arrest mandatory in writing for all offences under all statutes, para 66 there) and distinguishes *Shri Darshan* and *Sonam Raghuvanshi* (which applied a prejudice-oriented test to non-writing/inadequate-reasons cases, as opposed to total non-communication) | Fundamental Rights, Public law remedies/compensation |
| V. Senthil Balaji v. State (Dy. Director, ED) | (2024) 3 SCC 51 : 2023 SCC OnLine SC 934 | `Arrest-and-PMLA-Safeguards/V. Senthil Balaji v State (Deputy Director, ED).pdf` | Art. 226 writ of habeas corpus against a judicial remand order — not ordinarily maintainable as a matter of rule once detention becomes judicial, except where the statutory mandate (S.167 CrPC/S.19 PMLA) is **totally ignored** with total non-application of mind and a specific challenge is made; distinguished from a remand order with merely wrong/inadequate reasons, for which the only remedy is the statutory one, not Art.226 — a **reasoned/speaking** remand order (even if arguably deficient) must be challenged before the appropriate statutory forum, not by habeas corpus (paras 28-30); a challenge to remand "on merits" must proceed under the statute, while only non-compliance with a mandatory provision opens the extraordinary writ jurisdiction; on facts, appellant's production before the Principal Sessions Judge under S.19(3) PMLA rendered custody judicial, both remand orders showed clear application of mind, and rejection of his bail application meant the arrest/custody stood upheld regardless — habeas corpus not maintainable (paras 90-91); PMLA's sui generis scheme (S.19's rigorous pre-arrest procedure) held adequately complied with | PMLA, Habeas corpus, Remand |
